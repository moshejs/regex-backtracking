Why is my regex slow?

An innocent-looking pattern, a twenty-character string, and suddenly your CPU is doing millions of steps. Meet catastrophic backtracking — live.

Most of the time, a regular expression is fast — it reads your string once and answers. But some patterns hide a trap. Feed them the wrong input and the matching engine starts trying every possible way to split the string, and the number of ways grows exponentially. A pattern that looks harmless can take a modern computer longer than the age of the universe on a string you could type in a second.

What backtracking actually is

A backtracking engine matches a pattern by walking left to right and making guesses. When it hits a quantifier like a+ it greedily grabs as many as as it can, then tries to match the rest of the pattern. If that fails, it doesn't give up — it backtracks: gives one a back, and tries again. On a normal pattern there are only a handful of guesses to walk back through, so this is fast and it's what makes features like backreferences possible.

The step counter above is counting exactly those guesses. Each match attempt, each advance, each time the engine gives a character back and tries a different split — one step.

Why (a+)+ is a bomb

Look at the nesting: an inner a+ wrapped in an outer (...)+. Both are greedy, and they overlap — they both want to match runs of a. So for a string of n as, there are exponentially many ways to divide it between the inner and outer quantifiers: one group of five, or a group of two and a group of three, or five groups of one, and so on. That's 2n-1 distinct ways to carve up the string.

As long as the string matches, the engine finds a match on its first, greedy try and never explores the rest. The disaster is on failure. Add a single character the pattern can't match — here, the ! anchored against $ — and the final match fails. Now the engine dutifully backtracks and tries every one of those exponentially many splittings, because any of them might have let the rest succeed. None do. Twenty as and one ! is enough to ask for millions of steps; thirty would ask for billions.

How to spot the danger

The red flag is a quantifier inside a quantifier over the same characters: (a+)+, (a*)*, (.*)*, (\d+)*. Close cousins are overlapping alternations under a quantifier like (a|a)+ or (\w|\d)*, where the branches can match the same thing, so the engine has two indistinguishable choices at every step — and 2n combinations to try when the whole thing fails. The failure is what's expensive, which is exactly why these bugs sail through your tests (which use matching inputs) and only detonate when a user pastes something slightly off.

How real systems avoid it

Two families of fixes. The first keeps backtracking but bolts the trap shut: atomic groups (?>...) and possessive quantifiers a++ tell the engine "having matched this, never give it back," which collapses the exponential fan-out. Anchoring precisely and avoiding nested/overlapping quantifiers does the same by construction.

The second family abandons backtracking altogether. Engines like Google's RE2, Rust's regex crate, and Go's regexp compile the pattern to a finite automaton and simulate it, matching in time that's linear in the input length no matter what the pattern looks like — that's the flat gray line in the chart. They give up backreferences to get a guarantee: no input can ever make them blow up. If you're running regexes on untrusted input, that trade is almost always the right one. This whole page is a backtracking engine precisely so you can watch what those linear engines refuse to do.
